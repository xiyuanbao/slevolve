    Method overview¶

    This page summarises the formulation, the recursive modal state and its placement rule, the changes to the iteration starts, and where each change is in the code, and lists the core equations of the paper with every symbol explained.

    Formulation¶

    SLEvolve solves the gravitationally self-consistent sea-level equation on a spherically symmetric (1-D) viscoelastic Earth. It uses the normal-mode (Love-number) formulation of Kendall et al. (2005) as implemented in the legacy code of Han et al. (2022), called the legacy solver in this guide.

    The legacy solver evaluates the viscous response at every time step as an explicit sum over the whole load history. The cost per step therefore grows with the step number, and the total cost grows as O(N^2). SLEvolve replaces that sum with a recursive normal-mode state: one state per mode, degree and order, advanced by one decay factor per step. The cost per step is then independent of the length of the history, and the total cost is O(N). The recursion is exact. It covers all six history terms of the legacy solver: sea level, radial displacement, and the four degree-2 rotational terms.

    There are two implementations.

    • Fortran (fortran/src/). Derived from the legacy code.
    • Python (python/). Written from scratch following the same formulation, built on NumPy, Numba and ducc0.

    What is exact and what is not.

    • These parts are exact: with them, the paper's version (Zenodo v1.0.0, doi 10.5281/zenodo.23422438) reproduces the legacy solver to round-off (< 1e-11 m in final topography, identical inner-iteration counts and masks); the current code additionally applies the corrected floating-ice convention, the corrected sign of the axial rotation component m3 and the corrected sign of the order-1 rotation records (see Provenance):
    • the recursive state,
    • resident I/O,
    • the transform reductions,
    • the Legendre and FFT backends,
    • OpenMP.
    • These parts change only the starting point of an iteration, never the equations:
    • the extrapolated inner start,
    • the warm start across outer passes,
    • the partially relaxed initial topography.

    The converged result of each step then agrees with the legacy result within the inner tolerance epsilon1, not exactly.

    Core equations¶

    The equations below are those of the paper (Sect. 2), in its notation: \(\Delta\) denotes a change since the first time \(t_0\), \(\delta\) an increment over one step. Equation numbers are those of the paper.

    Sea-level equation (Eq. (1) of the paper)¶

    \[ \Delta S_j = \Delta\mathcal{SL}_j\,C_j\beta_j - T_0\,(C_j\beta_j-C_0\beta_0),\qquad \Delta\mathcal{SL}_j=\Delta\mathcal{G}_j-\Delta\mathcal{R}_j+\frac{\Delta\Phi_j}{g} \]
    • \(t_0<t_1<\dots<t_N\): the time steps; \(\delta t_j=t_j-t_{j-1}\) the step length (may be non-uniform).
    • \(\Delta S_j\): change of ocean height since \(t_0\) (the ocean load, as water thickness) at step \(j\).
    • \(\Delta\mathcal{SL}_j\): change of sea level (sea surface minus solid surface) since \(t_0\).
    • \(\Delta\mathcal{G}_j\): geographically variable part of the change of the sea-surface equipotential (geoid change).
    • \(\Delta\mathcal{R}_j\): change of the radial position of the solid surface (radial displacement).
    • \(\Delta\Phi_j/g\): the uniform term, a spatially constant shift of the sea surface fixed by conservation of ice-plus-ocean mass; \(g\) is the surface gravity.
    • \(C_j\): ocean function, 1 where sea level is positive (sea surface above the solid surface), 0 elsewhere.
    • \(\beta_j\): ice function, 0 where grounded ice covers the surface, 1 elsewhere. The marine check: marine ice counts as grounded only where its thickness exceeds the water depth times \(\rho_w/\rho_I\); thinner ice floats and is not part of the load. \(\beta_j\) is the grounded-ice mask of Kendall et al. (2005, eqs 43-44): it is 0 exactly where the grounded ice \(I^*_j\) is non-zero, so the ocean under floating ice shelves is part of the water-loading ocean \(C_j\beta_j\) and of the ocean area of the conservation term. A floating shelf together with the water beneath it weighs as much as a water column up to the sea surface, so a change of sea level under a shelf changes the load as in the open ocean. The flotation check uses the current topography iterate \(T_j^{(i)} = T_0 - \Delta\mathcal{SL}_j^{(i)}\) of the inner iteration (the topography that also defines the re-guessed ocean function); the first iterate of a step uses the converged topography of the previous step. Mask update rule: \(I^*_j\), its spectrum (the ice-load increment), \(\beta_j\) and \(C_j\beta_j\) are re-evaluated with every re-guess of the ocean function; the ice analysis is repeated only when the grounded flag of at least one cell changed, and the grounded mask is updated at most three times per step (a cell at flotation can otherwise make the mask alternate between two states); after that it is kept for the rest of the step. The viscous state is still advanced once per step with the converged load of the previous step. With the marine check off, \(I^*_j = I_j\) and only the mask definition applies.
    • \(T_0\): topography at \(t_0\); prescribed in a forward run, found by the outer iteration in an ice age run.

    The equation is nonlinear because \(C_j\) and \(\beta_j\) depend on the sea level being solved for. At each step an inner (Picard) iteration on the ocean-load increment \(\delta S_j\) runs until its relative change between iterates falls below \(\epsilon_1\) (default \(10^{-5}\)).

    Response to the load (Eqs. (2)-(5) of the paper)¶

    For each spherical-harmonic coefficient \((\ell,m)\):

    \[ \Delta\mathcal{SL}_{\ell m}(t_j)=T_\ell\left[E_\ell\,\Delta L_{\ell m}(t_j)+V_{\ell m}(t_j)\right],\qquad T_\ell=\frac{4\pi a^3}{M_e(2\ell+1)},\qquad E_\ell=1+k^E_\ell-h^E_\ell \]
    • \(a\), \(M_e\): radius and mass of the Earth.
    • \(\Delta L_{\ell m}(t_j)=\sum_{n=0}^{j}\delta L^n_{\ell m}\): cumulative load change; \(\delta L^n_{\ell m}=\rho_I\,\delta I^{*\,n}_{\ell m}+\rho_w\,\delta S^n_{\ell m}\) the load increment of step \(n\), with \(\delta I^{*}\) the increment of grounded ice thickness (marine ice included where grounded) and \(\rho_I\), \(\rho_w\) the ice and water densities.
    • \(E_\ell\): elastic sea-level response, from the elastic load Love numbers \(h^E_\ell\) (radial) and \(k^E_\ell\) (potential).
    • \(V_{\ell m}\): viscous (hereditary) response. At degree 2 the rotational feedback adds \(g^{-1}[E^T_2\,\Delta\Lambda_{2m}+V^T_{2m}]\), with the tidal Love numbers and the centrifugal potential perturbation \(\Lambda\), formed in the same way.

    The viscoelastic Love numbers are finite sums of decaying exponentials (Eq. (4)):

    \[ h_\ell(t)=h^E_\ell\,\delta(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{K_\ell} r^\ell_k\,e^{-s^\ell_k t},\qquad k_\ell(t)=k^E_\ell\,\delta(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{K_\ell} r^{\ell\prime}_k\,e^{-s^\ell_k t} \]
    • \(K_\ell\): number of modes at degree \(\ell\) (16 to 26 in the benchmark Earth models).
    • \(s^\ell_k>0\): inverse relaxation time of mode \(k\); \(r^\ell_k\), \(r^{\ell\prime}_k\): its residues (modal amplitudes) for radial displacement and potential; \(\delta(t)\): Dirac delta.

    A load increment persists after it is applied, so its viscous response is the time integral of the impulse response, \((r/s)(1-e^{-st})\) per mode: the response to a step load increment is the elastic term plus a sum over modes of \(a_k(1-e^{-s_k t})\). The full-history form of the viscous term is (Eq. (5))

    \[ V_{\ell m}(t_j)=\sum_{n=0}^{j-1}\beta_\ell(t_j-t_n)\,\delta L^n_{\ell m},\qquad \beta_\ell(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_\ell} a^\ell_k\left(1-e^{-s^\ell_k t}\right),\qquad a^\ell_k=\frac{r^{\ell\prime}_k-r^\ell_k}{s^\ell_k} \]
    • \(\beta_\ell(t)\): viscous step-response kernel of the sea level (not the ice function \(\beta_j\)).
    • \(a^\ell_k\): modal weight of mode \(k\) for sea level. The radial displacement uses the weights \(r^\ell_k/s^\ell_k\) of the same modes. This sum costs \(O(j)\) at step \(j\), \(O(N^2)\) in all; it is what the legacy solver evaluates (six such sums: sea level, radial displacement and four degree-2 rotational terms).

    Recursive modal state (Eqs. (8) and (10) of the paper)¶

    \[ V_{\ell m}(t_j)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_\ell}a^\ell_k\,R^k_{\ell m}(t_j),\qquad R^k_{\ell m}(t_j)=d^\ell_k(\delta t_j)\,R^k_{\ell m}(t_{j-1})+\bigl(1-d^\ell_k(\delta t_j)\bigr)\,\Delta L_{\ell m}(t_{j-1}),\qquad d^\ell_k(\delta t_j)=e^{-s^\ell_k\,\delta t_j} \]
    • \(R^k_{\ell m}(t_j)\): the modal state of mode \(k\), one field per mode, degree and order; \(R^k_{\ell m}(t_0)=0\). It is defined as \(\Delta L_{\ell m}(t_{j-1})-Q^k_{\ell m}(t_j)\) with \(Q^k_{\ell m}(t_j)=\sum_{n=0}^{j-1}e^{-s^\ell_k(t_j-t_n)}\delta L^n_{\ell m}\) (Eq. (7)).
    • \(d^\ell_k(\delta t_j)\): one-step decay factor of mode \(k\) over the step length \(\delta t_j\); a non-uniform step changes only \(d\).
    • The update is algebraically identical to the full-history sum for any sequence of increments; it costs one multiply-add per mode and coefficient per step, \(O(N)\) in all. The rotational terms carry their own states with the same update.

    Placement rule (Sect. 2.4 of the paper)¶

    An increment applied at \(t_j\) has zero viscous age at \(t_j\) (\(\beta_\ell(0)=0\)), so \(V_{\ell m}(t_j)\) depends only on the converged \(\Delta L(t_{j-1})\). Hence:

    1. advance-and-read: before the inner iteration of step \(j\), the states are advanced once with the update above and \(V_{\ell m}(t_j)\) is read from them; it stays fixed during the iteration;
    2. the inner iteration updates only the elastic and uniform terms, \(C_j\), \(\beta_j\) and \(\delta S_j\);
    3. commit: after convergence, \(\Delta L_{\ell m}(t_j)=\Delta L_{\ell m}(t_{j-1})+\delta L^j_{\ell m}\).

    Trial iterates never enter the stored history, so every iteration sees exactly the viscous response of the full-history sum, and both formulations take the same inner iterations.

    Second-order load placement (optional)¶

    The placement rule above is the step placement of Kendall et al. (2005): the increment of step \(j\) is applied at \(t_j\) as a step load, so its viscous response starts at \(t_j\). On average the response then lags by half a step, and the time stepping is first order in \(\delta t\). As an option, each increment can instead be taken to accrue linearly over its step \((t_{j-1},t_j]\) (a ramp). The viscous kernel integrated over the ramp is

    \[ \int_{t_{j-1}}^{t_j}\left(1-e^{-s(t-\tau)}\right)\frac{d\tau}{\delta t_j} =1-\varphi(s\,\delta t_j)\,e^{-s(t-t_j)},\qquad \varphi(x)=\frac{1-e^{-x}}{x}\quad(t\ge t_j) \]

    for each mode, so the ramp only replaces the unit amplitude of the decaying exponential by \(\varphi(s^\ell_k\delta t_j)\). \(\varphi(x)=1-x/2+x^2/6-\dots\) tends to 1 as \(\delta t_j\to0\) (the step response is recovered); the code uses this series below \(x=10^{-4}\). The elastic term and the direct sea-level terms (\(E_\ell\), the ocean function, the conservation term) are unchanged, because a ramp is complete at \(t_j\). The response is exact for a load that is piecewise linear in time and second order for a smooth load.

    In the recursion the state stays one field per mode, and the extra term is local to the step:

    1. advance-and-read is unchanged. The current increment has the viscous response \(\sum_k a^\ell_k c^\ell_k\,\delta L^j_{\ell m}\) at \(t_j\), with \(c^\ell_k=1-\varphi(s^\ell_k\delta t_j)\). It is linear in \(\delta L^j\), like the elastic term, so it enters the inner iteration as an addition to the elastic coefficient of the current increment: \(T_\ell[E_\ell\Delta L_{\ell m}+V_{\ell m}+C_\ell\,\delta L^j_{\ell m}]\), \(C_\ell=\sum_k a^\ell_k c^\ell_k\) (radial displacement: the radial weights);
    2. commit adds \(c^\ell_k\,\delta L^j_{\ell m}\) to every modal state \(R^k_{\ell m}\) before \(\Delta L\) is updated. One step later the increment contributes \(d\,c+(1-d)=1-d\,\varphi\), the ramp kernel, and the identity carries over to all later steps.

    \(c^\ell_k\) uses the length \(\delta t_j\) of the step in which the increment accrued, so non-uniform steps (a times file) need nothing else; the coefficients are recomputed once per distinct step length. The rotational states take the same two changes. The polar-motion equation becomes implicit in the current increment and is solved in closed form (\(i=1,2\)):

    \[ m_i=\frac{\alpha\left[\dfrac{(1+k^E_2)\,I_{i3}+\Sigma^I_{i3}+c_I\,\delta I_{i3}}{C-A}+\dfrac{\Sigma^m_i-c_m\,m_i^{\,j-1}}{k_f}\right]}{1-\alpha\,c_m/k_f}, \qquad \alpha=\frac{1}{1-k^{T,E}_2/k_f} \]

    with \(\Sigma^I\), \(\Sigma^m\) the read modal sums, \(c_I=\sum_k a^I_k c_k\) and \(c_m=\sum_k a^m_k c_k\) (degree-2 weights of the moment of inertia and of the tidal potential); and \(m_3=-[(1+k^E_2)\,I_{33}+\Sigma^I_{33}+c_I\,\delta I_{33}]/C\) (Mitrovica & Wahr 2011, eq. 15). The viscous tidal terms of sea level and radial displacement gain \(c_{tsl}\,\delta\Lambda\) and \(c_{trr}\,\delta\Lambda\). The cost is one multiply-add per mode and coefficient at the commit (the advance has three) and nothing in the inner iteration beyond one multiply-add per coefficient.

    The ramp removes the lag of the viscous response. Events that the step sequence resolves only to one step remain first order: a marine basin that floods in the step in which its ice cover vanishes (the shoreline term), and ice that grounds or floats. They leave a first-order remainder on margins and forebulges.

    Default off. The paper and the shipped reference/ results use the step placement of Kendall et al. (2005), so the default keeps it and every default output is unchanged. The option is the build switch RAMP=1 (Fortran) and --ramp (Python run_ais.py, run_outer.py). The method was derived and verified in a separate study.

    Analytic present-day rates (optional)¶

    Rates are read from the modal states at the end of a step (left derivative), the current increment taken to accrue linearly over the step. Inside the step every state obeys \(dR^k/dt=s_k(P-R^k)\), with \(P\) the cumulative driver, so

    \[ \frac{d\,\Delta\mathcal{SL}_{\ell m}}{dt}\Big|_{t_j}=T_\ell\left[E_\ell\frac{\delta L^j_{\ell m}}{\delta t_j} +\sum_k a^\ell_k s^\ell_k\left(\Delta L_{\ell m}(t_j)-R^k_{\ell m}(t_j)-c^\ell_k\,\delta L^j_{\ell m}\right)\right] \]

    (radial displacement: \(h^E_\ell\) and the radial weights), with \(R^k\) the state after the advance of step \(j\). The degree-2 rotational rates differentiate the polar-motion equation, which is explicit in \(\dot m\): the current-increment term becomes \(s_k(m-r^m_k)\) with the ramp-consistent state \(r^m_k+c_k\delta m\), \(\dot I=\delta I/\delta t_j\), and \(\dot\Lambda\) follows by the chain rule. Degree 0 of \(d\mathcal{SL}/dt\) is the rate of the conservation term on the ocean function \(C^*_j\) of the end of the step: \(\bigl(-(\rho_I/\rho_w)\,\delta I^*_{00}/\delta t_j-\overline{C^*_j\,\dot{\mathcal{SL}}}\bigr)/\overline{C^*_j}\) (overbar: global mean; \(\dot{\mathcal{SL}}\) without degree 0).

    A two-point difference of the outputs, \((X_j-X_{j-1})/\delta t_j\), is the mean rate over the last step, centred at \(t_j-\delta t_j/2\): first order in \(\delta t\) whatever the placement. The analytic read-out is the rate at \(t_j\). With the ramp the viscous memory is read without lag; what remains is that the current increment, in particular its ocean-load and grounded-ice parts, which the sea-level equation determines, is taken as linear over the last step. That term is first order, with a small constant: on a piecewise-linear ice history (synthetic Earth, \(L=256\), steps of 1 kyr to 125 yr) the analytic uplift rate, \(\dot J_2\) and polar-motion rate with the ramp were 8 to 100 times closer to the converged values than two-point differences at the same step. The read-out is also available with the step placement, where the history is that of the step placement and only the last increment is read as a ramp; it then inherits the lag of the step placement and was not more accurate than a two-point difference in that test, so use it with the ramp. The rates are output only (Fortran SLEVOLVE_RATES=1, Python --save-fields ...,dSLdt,dRdt or run_outer.py --rates; Output fields).

    Self-consistent axial rotation (optional)¶

    The axial component \(m_3\) of the rotation-vector perturbation \(\boldsymbol\omega=\Omega\,(m_1,m_2,1+m_3)\) follows from the axial Liouville equation. By default SLEvolve uses the form of Mitrovica & Wahr (2011, eq. 15):

    \[ m_3=-\frac{1}{C}\left[I^L_{33}+k^L_2*I^L_{33}\right] \]

    (\(*\): convolution in time; \(I^L_{33}\): inertia perturbation of the surface load; \(k^L_2\): degree-2 load Love number). Below that equation Mitrovica & Wahr (2011) note that it leaves out the part of \(I_{33}\) driven by the change in centrifugal potential, which they call orders of magnitude smaller than the loading part. For the equatorial components \(m_1,m_2\) that part is kept: it is the tidal term \(k^T_2*m/k_f\) of the polar-motion equation (Mitrovica et al. 2005, eq. 2; Mitrovica & Wahr 2011, eqs. 13-14). The option adds the axial counterpart:

    \[ C\,m_3=-\left[(1+k^L_2)*I^L_{33}+I^R_{33}+\tfrac13\,\Delta T^R\right] \]
    • \(I^R_{33}\): the change of \(I_{33}\) from the deformation of the Earth by the degree-2 centrifugal potential. By MacCullagh's formula for the solver's own \(\Lambda_{20}\), \(I^R_{33}=-\tfrac{2\sqrt5}{3}\,\dfrac{Ma}{g}\,k^T_2*\Lambda_{20}\) (\(k^T_2\): degree-2 tidal Love number of the Love-number file; \(M\), \(a\), \(g\): mass, radius and surface gravity). Linearised in \(m\) this is \(\tfrac43\,(\Omega^2a^5/3G)\,k^T_2*m_3\), the (3,3) element of the rotational inertia perturbation whose (1,3) and (2,3) elements are Mitrovica & Wahr (2011, eq. 13).
    • \(\tfrac13\Delta T^R\): the degree-0 part of the centrifugal potential, \(\Lambda_{00}=(a^2\Omega^2/3)(|m|^2+2m_3)\), expands the Earth uniformly. This changes the trace \(T\) of the inertia tensor, a third of which falls on \(I_{33}\), but not the exterior degree-2 potential (so not \(J_2\)). The code writes it as \(\tfrac13\Delta T^R=\tfrac32\,\tau_c\,C\,\Lambda_{00}/(a^2\Omega^2)\approx\tau_c\,C\,m_3\), with an axial number \(\tau_c\) of the Earth model. The Love-number files carry no degree-0 numbers, so the elastic value of PREM, \(\tau_c=5.36\times10^{-4}\), is used (Python --axial-tau-c, Fortran AXIAL_TAU_C to change it). Its viscous relaxation, about 3 %, is left out; that is at most 1.6e-5 of \(m_3\).

    In the Laplace domain (\(s\)), for the linearised equation,

    \[ m_3(s)=-\frac{1}{C}\,\frac{I^L_{33}(s)\,\bigl[1+k^L_2(s)\bigr]}{1+Q\,k^T_2(s)+\tau_c},\qquad Q=\frac{4}{9}\,\frac{\Omega^2a^5}{GC}=\frac43\,\frac{C-A}{C}\,\frac{1}{k_f} \]

    with \(k_f=3G(C-A)/(a^5\Omega^2)\) (Mitrovica et al. 2005, eq. 5). This is the axial counterpart of the equatorial denominator \(1-k^T_2(s)/k_f\) (Mitrovica et al. 2005, eq. 6; Mitrovica & Wahr 2011, eq. 17). The axial correction is positive, so the feedback is stabilising, and it is small: \(Q\,k^T_2=1.4\times10^{-3}\) for the elastic and \(4.3\times10^{-3}\) for the fluid-limit \(k^T_2\).

    Per-step solve. No new modal state is needed. The degree-2 states \(r_k\) of \(\Lambda_{20}\) are already advanced once per step for the tidal sea-level term, and the weights are the tidal residues \(a^m_k\) of the polar-motion equation. At the end of step \(j\)

    \[ k^T_2*\Lambda_{20}=(k^{T,E}_2+c_m)\,\Lambda_{20}^{\,j}+V^j-c_m\,\Lambda_{20}^{\,j-1},\qquad V^j=\sum_k a^m_k\,r_k \]

    (\(c_m=0\) with the default step placement). \(\Lambda\) is quadratic in \(m\), so \(m_3\) solves the scalar equation

    \[ C\,m_3+E\left[(k^{T,E}_2+c_m)\,\Lambda_{20}(m)+V^j-c_m\Lambda_{20}^{\,j-1}\right]+T_c\,\Lambda_{00}(m) +(1+k^E_2)\,I_{33}+\Sigma^I_{33}+c_I\,\delta I_{33}=0 \]

    with \(E=-\tfrac{2\sqrt5}{3}Ma/g\) and \(T_c=\tfrac32\tau_c C/(a^2\Omega^2)\) (full; 0 for deg2), by Newton iteration from the value of the default equation; two iterations reach round-off. In linear form its denominator is \(C[1+Q(k^{T,E}_2+c_m)+\tau_c]\), the axial counterpart of \(1-\alpha c_m/k_f\) above. The analytic rate (previous subsection) differentiates the same equation, which is linear in \(\dot m_3\); its viscous tidal part is \(\sum_k a^m_k s_k(\Lambda_{20}-r_k-c_k\,\delta\Lambda_{20})\) with the ramp-consistent states. The cost is one scalar Newton solve per inner iteration (about 1 µs); no transform, modal state or memory is added.

    Size. In the Case 3 ice age run (\(L=511\), ramp, analytic rates, five outer passes) the option lowers \(\dot m_3\) and the rate of change of the length of day by 0.41 % (deg2) and 0.49 % (full). \(\dot J_2\) from the geoid changes by 2e-5 relative, because the sea-level equation already contains the rotational deformation through \(\Lambda\); relative sea level changes by at most 11 µm (every fifth step, all grid cells), the uplift rate by at most 2.5 nm/yr, and the polar-motion speed and direction by less than 1e-8 relative and 1e-6 degrees. With the option on, the inertia route \(-3C\dot m_3/(2Ma^2)\) agrees with \(\dot J_2\) from the geoid to 8.5e-5 at the default inner tolerance (0.41 % with the option off); the remainder comes from \(m_3\) being formed from the ocean load of the previous inner iterate.

    Default off. The paper and the shipped reference/ results use Mitrovica & Wahr (2011, eq. 15), so the default keeps it and every default output is unchanged. The option is the build switch AXIAL_ROTATION=deg2|full (Fortran) and --axial-rotation deg2|full (Python run_ais.py, run_outer.py; keyword axial_rotation of the solver classes).

    Extrapolated inner start (Sect. 2.5 of the paper)¶

    \[ \delta S^{(0)}_j=\delta t_j\left[\dot S_{j-1}+\left(\dot S_{j-1}-\dot S_{j-2}\right)\frac{\delta t_j+\delta t_{j-1}}{\delta t_{j-1}+\delta t_{j-2}}\right],\qquad \dot S_i=\frac{\delta S_i}{\delta t_i} \]
    • \(\delta S^{(0)}_j\): first guess of the ocean-load increment of step \(j\) (superscript 0: initial iterate).
    • \(\dot S_i\): rate of the converged increment of step \(i\).
    • For uniform steps this is \(2\,\delta S_{j-1}-\delta S_{j-2}\). The first two steps use the eustatic guess (the mass of the ice increment spread uniformly over the ocean), which the legacy solver uses at every step. Only the starting point changes; the converged result agrees within \(\epsilon_1\).

    Outer iteration and partially relaxed initial topography (Eqs. (11) and (12) of the paper)¶

    \[ T_0\leftarrow T_0-\left(T_{\mathrm{pred}}-T_{\mathrm{obs}}\right) \]
    • \(T_{\mathrm{pred}}\): present topography predicted by a pass (a full forward run); \(T_{\mathrm{obs}}\): observed present topography. The misfit is the area-weighted RMS of \(T_{\mathrm{pred}}-T_{\mathrm{obs}}\).

    First-pass initial topography from the partially relaxed response:

    \[ T_0=T_{\mathrm{obs}}+\sum_i\mathcal{SL}^{\mathrm{seg}}_i,\qquad h^{\tau_i}_\ell=h^E_\ell+\sum_k\frac{r^\ell_k}{s^\ell_k}\left(1-e^{-s^\ell_k\tau_i}\right) \]
    • \(i\): monotonic segments of the global ice-volume history (split at its extrema; changes below 1 % of the volume range ignored). The superscript seg marks a quantity of one whole segment (neither a one-step increment δ nor a change since \(t_0\), Δ).
    • \(I^{\mathrm{seg}}_i\): net change of grounded ice over segment \(i\); \(\tau_i\): its mean age at the end of the run, weighted by the ice-volume change of each step.
    • \(h^{\tau_i}_\ell\), \(k^{\tau_i}_\ell\) (the latter with \(r^{\ell\prime}_k\)): Love numbers partially relaxed to age \(\tau_i\).
    • \(\mathcal{SL}^{\mathrm{seg}}_i\): sea-level response to \(I^{\mathrm{seg}}_i\) with these Love numbers; the sea-level equation is solved once for the summed response, with the water load evaluated with the segment-averaged Love numbers \(h^w_\ell=\sum_i w_i h^{\tau_i}_\ell\), \(w_i=|V^{\mathrm{seg}}_i|/\sum_j|V^{\mathrm{seg}}_j|\) (\(V^{\mathrm{seg}}_i\): net ice-volume change of segment \(i\)). Implemented in python/init_topo_segmented.py.

    Horizontal (tangential) displacement: convention¶

    For a spherically symmetric Earth the surface horizontal displacement is the surface gradient of a scalar, read out from the same modal states R_k as the radial displacement with different weights:

    Psi_lm(t_n) = T_l [ l^E_l L_lm(t_n) + sum_k (l_k / s_k) R_k,lm(t_n) ]          (l >= 1, metres)
            + delta_l2 [ l^T_2 Lambda_2m(t_n) + sum_k (l^T_k / s_k) R^Lambda_k,m(t_n) ] / g
u_theta = dPsi/dtheta ,   u_phi = (1 / sin theta) dPsi/dphi
    • T_l = 4 pi a^3 / (M (2l+1)) (eq. B16, the factor of the radial R), L_lm the total surface load (ice + ocean, kg m^-2) in the same instantaneous form as R (rhoi*deltaicestar + rhow*deltaS_prev + rhow*load_inc_lm), Lambda the rotational driving potential (same terms as the R rotation with h replaced by l). l^E_l = elast(2,l)/l, l_k = resl(k,l) (after the division by l), l^T_2 = telast(2,2)/2, l^T_k = tresl(k,2). So T_l L_lm is the load potential divided by g, and u_r = h (Phi/g) and u_h = l grad_1 (Phi/g) (grad_1 = dimensionless surface gradient).
    • theta is colatitude, so u_theta > 0 points south; u_north = -u_theta; u_phi > 0 points east. Grid rows run north to south and column 0 is phi = 0 (the solver grid).
    • With l > 0 the elastic horizontal motion points toward a positive load (away from a region of ice loss).
    • Coefficients are written in the spharmt (U) convention of dS_converged (both codes; Python converts from its raw-mode V convention).
    • Fortran: SLEVOLVE_HORIZ (0 default). The weights a_hl = l_k/s_k, a_thl are added to the spectrum only when the switch is on; modal_state_mod::state_read_horizontal reads the current states between advance-and-read and commit and does not change them. With 0 (or with any value) every other output is identical to the build without the feature.
    • The grid gradient is not computed in Fortran (the on-the-fly Legendre backend has no dP/dtheta table); it is formed from horiz<N>.npy by python/tools/horiz_grad.py (ducc0 spin-1 gradient synthesis, python/slevolve/horiz.py), which is also what Python --horizontal-grid uses.
    • Usage: Horizontal displacement output; checks: Horizontal displacement output.
